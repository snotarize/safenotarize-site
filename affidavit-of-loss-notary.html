What an Affidavit of Loss Is

An affidavit of loss is a statement made under oath or affirmation about an item or document the signer can no longer produce. It often explains what was lost, when and where the loss was discovered, the circumstances, identifying numbers, efforts to locate the item, whether a police or incident report was filed, and why a replacement or other action is requested.

The affidavit does not automatically replace the missing item. It supplies facts for the organization responsible for deciding what happens next. Banks, schools, employers, insurers, licensing offices, property managers, corporations, and government agencies may have different procedures and forms.

SafeNotarize notarizes an eligible completed affidavit. We do not verify that the item was lost, approve a replacement, prepare the statement, or decide whether the recipient should rely on it.

Common Situations Where It May Be Requested

A receiving organization may ask for an affidavit after a certificate, check, receipt, stock certificate, identification credential, title, permit, company document, school record, or other item cannot be found. The affidavit may also describe a document destroyed by fire, water, moving, theft, or another event.

Some losses have a specific official process. A lost valid U.S. passport, for example, must be reported through the Department of State’s prescribed process, including Form DS-64 or its online reporting option. A generic affidavit of loss is not automatically a substitute. Similar agency-specific rules may apply to driver licenses, vehicle titles, immigration documents, professional licenses, and financial instruments.

Before preparing an affidavit, ask the recipient for its current form and replacement checklist. Confirm whether a police report, account statement, proof of ownership, indemnity agreement, publication, bond, or other evidence is required.

Who Signs and What Information to Include

The person with knowledge of the loss normally signs as the affiant. That may be the owner, account holder, custodian, employee, authorized representative, or another person identified by the recipient. A representative may need separate proof of authority.

Use precise facts rather than guesses. Include the item’s official name, number, issue date, owner, issuing organization, last known location, date the loss was noticed, and steps taken to find or report it when the form requests those details. If the item may have been stolen, follow the recipient’s police-report or fraud instructions.

Review spelling, account or serial numbers, and dates carefully. Avoid placing unnecessary sensitive information into a generic affidavit. Use the secure form and delivery method required by the receiving organization.

Witnesses, ID, and Online Notarization

Some loss affidavits require only the affiant’s notarized signature; others may request witnesses, multiple owners, corporate authorization, or a special indemnity. Requirements vary. The notary does not serve as a document witness unless the roles and rules permit it.

The signer should have acceptable government-issued photo identification and complete remote identity verification. If the name on the affidavit differs from the current ID, ask the recipient what supporting evidence it expects. International signers should review the acceptable identification guide.

Online notarization may be available when the form, notarial act, signer, witnesses, platform, applicable law, and recipient permit it. Ask whether the recipient accepts an electronically notarized affidavit and electronic delivery.

Receiving-Party Acceptance and Common Mistakes

The receiving organization decides whether the affidavit is sufficient. It may reject a generic document, require a proprietary form, demand an original paper document, or require additional proof. Notarization does not compel an agency or company to replace the lost item.

Common mistakes include using an affidavit when an official report form is required, failing to identify the item precisely, leaving unexplained date gaps, signing before the notarial session, omitting joint owners, using a mismatched name, failing to report suspected theft, or submitting the affidavit to the wrong department.

Do not state that an item was stolen unless that is accurate. Do not use the affidavit to avoid an official cancellation, fraud, or replacement process. If the loss involves significant financial value, disputed ownership, identity theft, or potential liability, obtain appropriate professional advice.

Affidavit of Loss FAQ

Practical answers about document preparation, participants, and online notarization.

What details are normally included in an affidavit of loss?

The recipient may request the item description, identifying number, owner, date and location of loss, circumstances, search efforts, reporting steps, and replacement request. Use its current form and instructions.

Is a notarized loss affidavit enough to replace a missing document?

Not always. The issuing or receiving organization may require its own application, fees, police report, bond, supporting records, cancellation process, or in-person step.

Can I use a loss affidavit instead of Form DS-64 for a passport?

Do not assume so. The U.S. Department of State provides a specific process for reporting a valid passport lost or stolen. Follow the official instructions.

May two owners sign the same affidavit of loss online?

Multiple signers may be possible when the form and recipient allow it. Include every signer during booking; additional signer fees and separate identity verification may apply.

What if the item was stolen rather than misplaced?

Follow the recipient’s theft, fraud, cancellation, and police-report instructions. Describe the facts accurately and do not rely on notarization as a substitute for required reporting.

Can an employee sign for a company’s lost document?

The recipient may require proof that the employee or representative has authority to sign. Confirm the correct signer and any business authorization documents.

Will the online notary determine whether my loss explanation is sufficient?

No. The notary verifies identity and performs the requested notarial act. The recipient decides whether the facts and supporting evidence are sufficient.

Can I notarize an affidavit of loss after finding the item?

Contact the receiving organization before proceeding. A statement that the item remains lost may no longer be accurate, and an item already reported lost or canceled may have separate rules.
