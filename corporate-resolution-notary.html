What a Corporate Resolution Generally Records

A corporate resolution is a written record of an action attributed to a corporation’s board of directors, shareholders, committee, or other authorized decision-making body. Resolutions may address opening or changing a bank account, borrowing money, approving a contract, buying or selling property, authorizing an officer, changing signers, approving a vehicle transaction, or confirming another business action.

The resolution is a company record. Corporate records and recipient instructions determine authorization. Does a corporate resolution need notarization? Not always. Some resolutions remain internal company records, while a bank, lender, title company, agency, or other receiving institution may request notarization for a particular transaction. SafeNotarize performs only the requested notarial act on an eligible completed document.

Some resolutions are signed by directors or shareholders. Others are certified by a secretary or acknowledged by an officer who will carry out the authorized transaction. Confirm the required format before the session because the recipient may expect a certified copy, a secretary’s certificate, a separate incumbency certificate, or its own resolution form.

Corporations, Shareholders, and Board-Approved Authority

A corporation is a legal entity separate from its individual shareholders, directors, and officers. Owning shares does not automatically mean that one shareholder may sign every company document. Likewise, an officer title does not allow the notary to assume that the person has authority for the specific transaction.

Board-approved authority may be documented through minutes, written consents, resolutions, officer appointments, or other governance records. Official Florida materials distinguish officers, board actions, shareholder actions, and corporate records; other states may use different rules.

The notary identifies the individual who appears and records the person’s stated representative capacity when the notarial certificate requires it. The notary does not review quorum, notice, voting percentages, conflicts, bylaws, or whether the resolution was properly adopted. Banks, lenders, title companies, agencies, investors, and contracting parties may separately request evidence of authority.

Who Commonly Signs a Corporate Resolution

The signer may be a president, vice president, secretary, treasurer, director, chair, authorized officer, or another representative named in the completed document. A secretary may certify that a resolution appears in the corporation’s records, while another officer may acknowledge a signature authorizing a transaction. Some documents require multiple corporate signatures.

The individual signer appears before the notary using personal identification, even when signing for the corporation. The signature block should show the corporation’s legal name, signer name, title, and representative capacity.

SafeNotarize does not determine who should sign. Ask the receiving organization and the company’s authorized adviser which officer, director, secretary, or other representative must execute or certify the document.

Certified Copies Versus Notarized Signatures

A certified corporate record and a notarized signature serve different purposes. A corporate secretary or another authorized record custodian may certify that a copy matches a resolution maintained in company records. A notary, by contrast, performs a notarial act for the individual signer, such as taking an acknowledgment or administering an oath or affirmation for a sworn statement.

Notarizing the secretary’s signature does not prove that the resolution is authentic, current, complete, or validly adopted, or that an officer still holds the stated position. A recipient may request an incumbency certificate, certified resolution, public records, or other evidence.

Do not substitute a notarized photocopy or personal statement when the bank, lender, title company, or agency requires a specific corporate certification. Obtain the exact requested document before booking.

Identification, Witnesses, and Online Notarization

The signer should present acceptable government-issued photo identification and complete the platform’s identity-verification steps. The name on the identification should reasonably match the individual name shown in the signature and notarial certificate. Review the acceptable ID for online notarization guide if the signer has an international passport, a name variation, or questions about expiration.

Whether witnesses are requested depends on the document, governing law, transaction, and receiving organization. A notary and a document witness are different roles. A witness does not become unnecessary merely because a signature is notarized, and a notary should not be counted as a document witness unless the applicable process permits it. See the online notary with witnesses guide before adding participants.

Remote online notarization may be available when the document, signer, notarial act, platform, and recipient permit it. Customers outside the United States should also review the international online notary guide.

Receiving-Party Acceptance and Common Mistakes

Recipients often control the practical requirements. A bank may provide a resolution template and signature card. A lender may request a borrowing resolution, incumbency evidence, or guaranty. A title company may ask for authority to sign a deed or closing document. A government office may require its own authorization form. Confirm whether the recipient accepts remote online notarization, electronic signatures, printed electronic records, and the chosen notarial certificate.

Common mistakes include using the wrong corporate name, omitting the signer’s title, leaving terms blank, signing too early, attaching an uncertified copy when certification is required, using an outdated resolution, and assuming notarization proves board approval.

The resolution should be complete before the notary session. SafeNotarize does not add corporate decisions, fill governance terms, validate votes, or advise whether the company should approve the transaction. Acceptance is not guaranteed.

Corporate Resolution Notary FAQ

Answers to common preparation and online-notarization questions.

Can a corporate resolution be notarized online?

It may be notarized online when the completed document, signer, requested notarial act, platform, applicable law, and receiving organization permit remote online notarization.

Does every corporate resolution require notarization?

No. Some resolutions are maintained only as internal records, while a bank, lender, title company, agency, or transaction recipient may request notarization for a particular use.

Can a shareholder sign a corporate resolution for the company?

A shareholder should sign only when the prepared document, corporate records, governing documents, and recipient requirements authorize that person to sign. Share ownership alone does not let the notary determine authority.

Does the notary confirm that the board approved the resolution?

No. The notary verifies the appearing signer and performs the notarial act. The notary does not validate notice, quorum, voting, minutes, written consents, or board approval.

Is a certified corporate resolution the same as a notarized resolution?

No. Certification generally concerns the corporation’s records, while notarization concerns an individual signature, acknowledgment, oath, or affirmation. A recipient may request both.

Can two corporate officers sign during separate online sessions?

Separate sessions may be possible, but the document, certificate wording, platform, and recipient must support separate execution. Confirm the process before the first signer begins.

What if the bank provides its own corporate resolution form?

Use the bank’s current form and instructions. Complete required business and transaction information before the session, and confirm which officer or secretary must sign.

Should the corporation name include Inc. or Corp. in the signature block?

Use the exact legal name shown in the prepared document and recipient records, including the entity suffix when applicable. The notary does not correct the company name.
