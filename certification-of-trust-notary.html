What Is a Certification of Trust?

A certification of trust—sometimes called a trust certificate or certificate of trust—is a document stating selected facts about an existing trust. It may identify the trust, settlor, current trustee, trustee powers, revocability, cotrustee signing authority, and the manner in which trust property is titled. The required content varies by state and transaction.

The certification is not the complete trust agreement and generally is not a substitute for every trust record. A recipient may request excerpts, amendments, identity documents, resolutions, or its own certification form. A certification is also different from a sworn affidavit unless it contains a jurat or sworn statement.

SafeNotarize does not prepare certifications, review the trust, determine trustee powers, confirm that amendments are complete, or provide estate, tax, banking, or legal advice. We notarize eligible signatures on a completed document.

When a Certification of Trust Is Commonly Presented

Trustees commonly present certifications when a transaction recipient needs evidence of specified trust facts without reviewing every dispositive term.

🧭

Banking and financial accounts

A bank or investment provider may request a certification before opening, retitling, or administering a trust account.

🏥

Real estate and title transactions

A title company, lender, recorder, or closing professional may request trustee and signing-authority information for trust property.

👥

Insurance, vehicles, and other assets

An insurer, agency, or business may ask for trust information before changing ownership or recognizing a trustee.

🗂️

Contract and business transactions

A counterparty may request a certification to identify the trustee and confirm the stated transaction authority.

Signing and witnesses

Who Signs and How Trustee Capacity Is Shown

A currently acting trustee normally signs the certification in an individual name while indicating representative capacity, such as “Jane Doe, Trustee.” If there are cotrustees, the trust, state law, certification, or recipient may determine whether one, several, or all must sign. The notary does not decide who has authority.

The signer is an individual even when acting for a trust. The notarial certificate may identify representative capacity, but notarization does not prove the trust exists, that the signer is the valid trustee, or that the trustee has the stated power. The recipient may separately review trust excerpts or institutional records.

Witnesses are not universally required for a certification of trust. They may be requested by a particular form, transaction, or jurisdiction. Follow the prepared document and recipient instructions.

Prepare carefully

Identification and Trust-Name Consistency

Each signing trustee should prepare acceptable government-issued photo identification. The individual name on the ID should be reasonably consistent with the signer name. The trust name, execution date, trustee title, and property or account information should match the records used for the transaction.

Review the acceptable ID guide. Before the session, check all statements, dates, trustee addresses, cotrustee provisions, attachments, signature blocks, acknowledgments or jurats, and recipient-specific language. The notary cannot fill in missing trust facts.

Online Notarization and Recipient Review

A certification of trust may be eligible for online notarization when the notarial act is authorized, the trustees can complete identity verification, and the recipient accepts remote execution. The notary witnesses or acknowledges the individual signature as required but does not compare the certification with the underlying trust.

Banks, lenders, title companies, government agencies, and closing professionals may use proprietary forms or request additional excerpts. Ask the recipient whether it accepts an electronic certification and remote notarization, whether all trustees must sign, and whether a paper original or recording is required.

Review the online-notary process, the witness guide, and the Durable Power of Attorney guide when a separate agent—not a trustee—is signing under POA authority.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Review the completed document and recipient instructions before the online session.

⚠️

Using an outdated trustee list

Confirm current trustees, successor appointments, resignations, and amendments before presenting the certification.

⚠️

Confusing the trust name with the trustee name

The trust is not the person appearing before the notary. The individual trustee signs in the required representative capacity.

⚠️

Assuming the recipient accepts any format

A bank or title company may require its own certification, date range, excerpts, or wet-ink original.

⚠️

Asking the notary to verify trust authority

Identity verification and notarization do not prove trustee status, trust validity, or transaction authority.

Certification of Trust FAQ

Answers to common preparation and online-notarization questions.

Is a certification of trust the same as the trust agreement?

No. A certification generally summarizes selected trust information. The trust agreement contains the complete governing terms and may still be requested in limited circumstances.

Does every trustee need to sign the certification?

That depends on the trust, certification language, applicable law, and recipient. Ask the bank, title company, lender, or other recipient whose signatures it requires.

Can the notary confirm that I am the current trustee?

No. The notary verifies the individual signer’s identity and performs the notarial act. Trustee status and authority are matters for the records and recipient.

May a bank require its own certification-of-trust form?

Yes. Financial institutions and transaction recipients may use proprietary forms or request additional excerpts and supporting documents.

Is a certification of trust always sworn under oath?

No. Some certifications use an acknowledgment, some may contain sworn statements, and others may have different execution language. The prepared notarial certificate controls.

Can a certification of trust be recorded with real estate records?

Recording practices and transaction requirements vary. Ask the title company, closing professional, or recorder whether recording is required and what format is accepted.

What if the trust was recently amended?

The certification should be current and accurate. The trustee and qualified advisers should determine whether amendments affect any statement or signing authority.

Will online notarization prove that the trust is valid?

No. Online notarization authenticates the signature for the notarial act. It does not validate the trust, its amendments, or the trustee’s powers.
